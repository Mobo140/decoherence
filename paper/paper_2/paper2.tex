\begin{abstract}
We extend the physics-informed residual BiLSTM framework of
\cite{Paper1Brusnikin} to two-qubit systems with XXZ and TFIM
Hamiltonians under unknown time-dependent dissipation.
We identify two root causes of poor initial 2-qubit performance:
(i)~the OLS physics prior is structurally wrong when coherence
oscillates (TFIM); (ii)~100 training trajectories per scenario is
insufficient.
We address both: an \emph{adaptive prior} gates the physics estimate
off when its own OLS $R^2 < 0.7$, and training is scaled to 500
trajectories.
On the XXZ model (scenario~D), the \texttt{transformer} achieves
$R^2 = 0.897$ (single-scenario) and $R^2 = 0.758$ (cross-system,
$N = 849$); the \texttt{physics\_lstm} with adaptive prior and improved
training (weighted BCE, noise augmentation) reaches $R^2 = 0.740$ and
AUROC $= 0.789$ in cross-system evaluation.
On the TFIM model (scenario~E), the \texttt{transformer} achieves
$R^2 = 0.689$ (single-scenario) and $R^2 = 0.599$ (cross-system, $N =
780$); the improved \texttt{physics\_lstm} reaches $R^2 = 0.575$
cross-system (up from $R^2 = -0.381$ baseline, $\Delta = +0.96$).
Measurement noise up to $\sigma = 0.10$ does not systematically degrade
accuracy: paired within seeds, $R^2$ changes by $-0.008 \pm 0.069$ with
the sign varying, a milder degradation than the single-qubit case
($\Delta R^2 = 0.23$ in \cite{Paper1Brusnikin}).
A sweep of the TFIM coupling constant $J$, replicated across three
seeds, shows predictability falling as $J/h$ grows---from
$R^2 = 0.72 \pm 0.03$ at $J = 0.2$ to $0.53$ on average for
$J \geq 0.8$---and then saturating.
A single sweep had suggested a dip localised at the quantum phase
transition with recovery beyond it; that structure does not survive
replication, and the transition point itself is unremarkable.
We offered two mechanisms for the decline and tested both---parity-sector
mixing by the dissipator, and a two-mode superposition from generic
initial states---and report that neither survives, leaving the trend as
an empirical result without an established cause.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In \cite{Paper1Brusnikin} we established that a physics-informed residual
BiLSTM achieves $R^2 = 0.94$--$0.98$ for single-qubit decoherence time
prediction under unknown time-dependent dissipation.
The architecture embeds the OLS-slope formula as a structural prior and
learns only the residual correction $\delta T$, ensuring analytical
recovery in the constant-$\gamma$ limit while being expressive for
complex $\gamma(t)$ profiles.

In this paper we ask: does the same approach scale to two-qubit systems?
This is a non-trivial extension for three reasons.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Non-exponential coherence.}
    For a two-qubit system with interaction Hamiltonian $H_\mathrm{int}$
    and per-qubit amplitude damping, the $\ell_1$-coherence is
    \emph{not} a simple exponential.
    Coherent oscillations (e.g., in the TFIM model) can make $C(t)$
    non-monotone over the observation window, breaking the OLS assumption.
  \item \textbf{Richer feature space.}
    Two-qubit observables include all 15 two-body Pauli correlators
    $\langle\sigma_\alpha \otimes \sigma_\beta\rangle$, providing more
    information but also more noise.
  \item \textbf{Entanglement-mediated decoherence.}
    Inter-qubit coupling ($J$ in both XXZ and TFIM) creates an
    additional decay channel that depends on the joint state and has no
    analogue in the single-qubit formula.
    Two-qubit entanglement can moreover vanish in finite time under
    purely local damping---the ``entanglement sudden death'' effect
    \cite{YuEberly2009}---so the joint decay need not follow the
    asymptotic exponential of either qubit alone.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Scenarios}

We study two two-qubit scenarios (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios2}).
Both use per-qubit $\sigma_-$ jump operators with the same Bernstein
$\gamma(t)$ parameterisation and shape diversity as \cite{Paper1Brusnikin}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Base architecture}

We use the same residual structure as \cite{Paper1Brusnikin}:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{T}_{2,\mathrm{rem}}
  = T_{\mathrm{physics}}(\mathbf{w})
  + \delta T_{\mathrm{LSTM}}(\mathbf{w}),
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
with a BiLSTM \cite{HochreiterSchmidhuber1997} (hidden size 128, 2
layers) processing the full two-qubit observable set: all 15 Pauli
correlators, $\ell_1$-coherence, purity, and their $\log$ transforms.
The regression and risk heads are unchanged from \cite{Paper1Brusnikin}.
Risk-head quality is reported throughout as the area under the ROC
curve (AUROC) \cite{Fawcett2006}.

\subsection{Adaptive prior}
\label{sec:adaptive}

The OLS physics prior assumes $\log C(t)$ is linear in $t$, which holds
for single-qubit amplitude damping but fails when coherence oscillates
(TFIM Hamiltonian with transverse field).
We introduce an \emph{adaptive prior gate}:
\begin{equation}
  T_{\mathrm{physics}}(\mathbf{w}) =
  \begin{cases}
    -1/\hat{s} - t_\mathrm{obs} & \text{if } R^2_\mathrm{OLS} \geq \tau \text{ and } \hat{s} < 0,\\
    0 & \text{otherwise,}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:adaptive}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{s}$ is the OLS slope of $\log C$ over the window and
$R^2_\mathrm{OLS}$ is the coefficient of determination of that fit.
When $R^2_\mathrm{OLS} < \tau = 0.7$, the physics estimate is
discarded and the LSTM residual head predicts $T_2$ directly.
The gate is applied identically at training and inference time, so the
model learns under the same signal it will receive at deployment.

\paragraph{Ablation variants.}
\texttt{physics\_only}: the bare OLS formula, \emph{without} the adaptive gate
(the gate of Eq.~\ref{eq:adaptive} is a property of the residual model, not of
the analytic baseline).
\texttt{lstm\_only}: BiLSTM without any physics prior.
\texttt{physics\_lstm}: full residual model with adaptive prior (ours).
\texttt{transformer}: pure Transformer encoder \cite{Vaswani2017} (same
as \cite{Paper1Brusnikin}, architecturally analogous to the Transformer
used in \cite{TransformerLindblad2025} for the related inverse
$\gamma(t)$-reconstruction task).

\paragraph{How to read the \texttt{physics\_only} row.}
Its $R^2$ values ($-0.687$ on D, $-4.267$ on E) should be read as
\emph{``the analytic formula does not apply here''} rather than as a
calibrated measure of how bad it is, because their magnitude is governed
by the clamp $t_\mathrm{max}$ rather than by the physics.
The estimator $T_2 = -1/\hat{s}$ is a hyperbola: it diverges as the
fitted slope $\hat{s} \to 0^-$, and the implementation returns
$t_\mathrm{max}$ outright when $\hat{s} \geq 0$.
Two-qubit coherence oscillates and plateaus, so near-zero slopes are
common, and each such window contributes an error of order
$t_\mathrm{max}$; since $R^2$ is a squared-error metric, a small minority
of windows dominates it.
In our diagnostics a handful of saturated windows accounted for the bulk
of the degradation, and sweeping the clamp moved $R^2$ by more than an
order of magnitude while leaving the median error unchanged.
MAE, which is not dominated by this tail, tells the proportionate story:
\texttt{physics\_only} is roughly $3\times$ (D) and $3.8\times$ (E) worse
than the Transformer, not unboundedly so.
We keep the row because it is the ablation that motivates the whole
paper---the physics prior is structurally wrong for two qubits---but the
$R^2$ magnitude itself should not be over-interpreted.

\paragraph{Run-to-run spread.}
The levels in Table~\ref{tab:e1_2} come from single runs.
While preparing this version we found three separate reproducibility
defects: the training path did not seed the ML framework's RNG, the
simulator drew each trajectory's initial pure state from an unseeded
global RNG, and the experiment scripts constructed their training
commands directly, bypassing the seeded path even after the first two
were fixed.
All three are now closed, and repeated runs of the experiment suite are
byte-identical for a given state of the trajectory cache.
That qualification matters: the simulator draws each trajectory's
initial pure state from one seeded stream, so how much of that stream a
run consumes depends on which trajectories it finds cached.
A cold cache---the state from which an independent replication
starts---and a fully populated one are each deterministic; a partially
populated cache, such as one left behind by an interrupted run, is not,
and we measured differences of order $0.015$ in $R^2$ from this alone.
Making trajectory content depend on the cache key rather than on stream
position would remove the caveat, at the cost of regenerating every
number in this paper.
To quantify what the unseeded runs cost, we replicated this ablation
across three seeds at the \emph{same} configuration as
Table~\ref{tab:e1_2} ($500$~trajectories, $150$~epochs), so levels are
directly comparable.
Every value in Table~\ref{tab:e1_2} falls inside the replicated spread,
but two of the gaps the table appears to show do not survive it
(mean~$\pm$~s.d.\ over three seeds):
on XXZ, \texttt{lstm\_only} reaches $0.633 \pm 0.060$ against
$0.599 \pm 0.120$ for \texttt{physics\_lstm}; on TFIM,
\texttt{lstm\_only} reaches $0.422 \pm 0.171$ against
$0.245 \pm 0.356$ for \texttt{physics\_lstm}.
The Transformer is best on both scenarios with the smallest spread
($0.843 \pm 0.028$ on D, $0.699 \pm 0.045$ on E), and
\texttt{physics\_only} stays far negative with a very large spread
($-3.98 \pm 4.31$ and $-5.98 \pm 4.09$), consistent with the clamp
behaviour described above.
The architecture conclusion is therefore robust; the
\texttt{physics\_lstm}/\texttt{lstm\_only} ordering is not.
robust; gaps of order $0.04$, however, are not.

\paragraph{Key findings.}
With 500 trajectories and the adaptive prior, scenario~E (TFIM) becomes
learnable: \texttt{lstm\_only} reaches $R^2 = 0.377$ and
\texttt{physics\_lstm} reaches $R^2 = 0.455$.
Both are dramatically improved over the 100-trajectory baseline
($R^2 < 0$ for all variants).

The \texttt{transformer} is the strongest single-scenario model:
$R^2 = 0.897$ on D and $R^2 = 0.689$ on E.
For XXZ, the Transformer's self-attention can exploit the structured
multi-qubit correlations that the OLS prior misses.
For TFIM, the absence of a physics prior is an advantage: the Transformer
has no misleading starting point to overcome.

Between \texttt{physics\_lstm} and \texttt{lstm\_only} this table
resolves nothing on either scenario.
The single run suggests that the prior helps on TFIM ($0.455$ vs.\
$0.377$) and slightly hurts on XXZ ($0.636$ vs.\ $0.673$), and an
earlier version of this paper explained the TFIM figure by the adaptive
gate discarding the physics estimate on oscillatory windows.
The three-seed replication does not support that reading: on TFIM the
ordering reverses in the mean ($0.245 \pm 0.356$ for
\texttt{physics\_lstm} against $0.422 \pm 0.171$ for
\texttt{lstm\_only}), and on XXZ the two overlap
($0.599 \pm 0.120$ against $0.633 \pm 0.060$).
Both gaps sit well inside the seed-to-seed spread, so we withdraw the
gate explanation and read the comparison as ``no resolvable difference
either way''.
What the table does establish is that \emph{some} learned component is
essential---every variant with one beats \texttt{physics\_only} by
orders of magnitude---not that the prior earns its place alongside it.

Read alone, this table suggests near-perfect insensitivity: $R^2$ spans
$0.007$ across the full range and AUROC $0.013$, and AUROC even
\emph{increases} slightly with noise, which we had attributed to small
additive noise acting as implicit regularisation for the risk head.
Those readings do not survive replication, though the robustness itself
does.

Across three seeds the levels reproduce---$R^2 = 0.496 \pm 0.168$ at
$\sigma = 0$ against $0.537$ here---but the $0.007$ span does not: the
per-seed spans are $0.072$, $0.148$ and $0.119$, an order of magnitude
wider, so this run's flatness was a fortunate draw rather than a
property of the model.
The apparent AUROC gain reverses as well: paired within each seed,
AUROC \emph{falls} from $\sigma = 0$ to $\sigma = 0.10$ in all three
($-0.059 \pm 0.050$), so the regularisation reading is withdrawn.

What robustness means here is therefore narrower, and we state it as the
paired comparison the claim needs: going from clean data to
$\sigma = 0.10$ changes $R^2$ by $-0.008 \pm 0.069$, with the sign
varying between seeds ($-0.072$, $-0.017$, $+0.065$).
Measurement noise up to $\sigma = 0.10$ does not systematically degrade
regression accuracy---it moves $R^2$ about as much as changing the seed
does ($0.113$ against $0.139$), which is the honest statement and still
a useful one.
It remains a milder degradation than the single-qubit case
($\Delta R^2 = 0.23$ in \cite{Paper1Brusnikin}), plausibly because the
15 Pauli correlator channels average noise more effectively, but we no
longer quantify the gap with a single run's span.

For TFIM, $R^2 = 0.566$ is close to E7a's $0.599$ while AUROC improves

\emph{and} in evaluation protocol, so the pair cannot isolate any of
them.

seeds, the shorter window wins on both metrics and on both scenarios,
in every one of the six paired comparisons:
$\Delta R^2 = +0.058 \pm 0.028$ (XXZ) and $+0.199 \pm 0.014$ (TFIM),
with $\Delta\mathrm{AUROC} = +0.045$ and $+0.100$.
There is no trade-off to explain---window $20$ is simply better than
window $50$ here, most markedly on TFIM.
The remaining TFIM $R^2$ ceiling still reflects the difficulty of
predicting decoherence time from oscillatory two-qubit coherence
windows, but it is not the price of the AUROC gain.

\paragraph{Does evaluating on the training configurations flatter E11a?}
E11a scores the model on the same configurations it trained on,
regenerating their trajectories under a different seed, so it sees fresh
noise realisations of systems it has already seen rather than unseen
systems.
This is worth checking rather than assuming, because the configuration
fixes most of $T_2$ on XXZ: the spread of $T_2$ within one configuration
is $0.25$--$0.53$ against $1.90$ across configurations (on TFIM the two
are comparable, $0.73$--$1.50$ against $1.46$), so a model that could
recognise a configuration from its window would recover much of the
target without generalising.
We therefore scored each trained model twice, once on its training
configurations and once on disjoint configurations drawn from a
different generator seed, with everything else held fixed.
The difference is nil: $\Delta R^2 = -0.000 \pm 0.009$ on XXZ and
$+0.015 \pm 0.059$ on TFIM (three seeds, sign mixed on both).
Configuration overlap does not inflate these numbers, and the
cross-system results stand as reported.

\paragraph{Summary of cross-system improvements.}
Two different baselines are relevant here, and we state each explicitly
to avoid conflating them.
Relative to the \emph{100-trajectory pilot} (D $R^2 = 0.523$,

XXZ $R^2: 0.523 \to 0.817$ ($\Delta = +0.294$) and

$0.692 \to 0.888$ ($\Delta = +0.196$).
AUROC is not reported for the pilot, so the AUROC deltas are quoted
against E6c rather than the pilot.

\paragraph{Note on E10b.}
An intermediate experiment (E10b) used window $L=30$, which reduced
the effective sample count and degraded $R^2$ while improving AUROC
moderately.
E11a supersedes E10b by recovering the sample count and applying

Should a $\tau$ effect exist, it is smaller than the run-to-run
variation of this pipeline, and resolving it would need more seeds
rather than a finer grid.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Status of the claims carried over from the single-qubit study.}
The single-qubit work \cite{Paper1Brusnikin} advanced four claims that
transfer to the two-qubit setting.
We state their outcome here explicitly, including where they fail.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Residual architecture (refuted for two qubits).}
    In the single-qubit case the physics-informed residual dominated
    both a pure-LSTM and a physics-only baseline.
    Here it does not: the Transformer beats \texttt{physics\_lstm} on
    both scenarios, and by a wide margin ($0.897$ vs.\ $0.636$ on XXZ,
    $0.689$ vs.\ $0.455$ on TFIM).
    Its standing against \texttt{lstm\_only} is less clear-cut: the
    tabulated gaps ($0.636$ vs.\ $0.673$ on XXZ, $0.455$ vs.\ $0.377$
    on TFIM) are of order $0.04$--$0.08$, whereas a three-seed
    replication puts the seed-to-seed standard deviation of
    \texttt{physics\_lstm} at $0.31$ (XXZ) and $0.76$ (TFIM); we
    therefore do not claim a difference in either direction there.
    The negative result that does survive replication is against the
    Transformer, and the reason---a structurally wrong prior on
    non-exponential coherence---is analysed below.
  \item \textbf{Early-window warning (holds for XXZ only).}
    The target was risk AUROC $\geq 0.92$.
    E11a reaches $0.937$ on XXZ but $0.888$ on TFIM, so the claim holds
    for XXZ and falls short for TFIM.
  \item \textbf{Noise robustness (stability yes, absolute level no).}
    Noise up to $\sigma = 0.10$ does not systematically degrade $R^2$
    ($-0.008 \pm 0.069$ paired within seeds, sign varying), a milder
    degradation than the single-qubit case ($\Delta R^2 = 0.23$),
    though not the near-perfect flatness a single run suggested.
    The absolute AUROC of the D+E mixture model sits at
    $0.813$--$0.826$ in that run and $0.70$--$0.84$ across seeds, below
    the $\geq 0.90$ bar the single-qubit study met.
    Robust, but not yet accurate enough.
  \item \textbf{Cross-system generalisation (partially confirmed).}
    A single E11a checkpoint does serve both Hamiltonians without
    retraining, and XXZ reaches $R^2 = 0.817$; but TFIM stalls at
    $R^2 = 0.566$, short of the $0.70$ target set for this work.
    The finite-size analysis below argues this ceiling is structural
    rather than a training deficiency; that argument is consistent with
    the replicated $J$-sweep but is not tested by it.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{When does the physics prior help vs.\ hurt in 2-qubit systems?}
The E6a results reveal that for 2-qubit systems, the Transformer
consistently outperforms \texttt{physics\_lstm} (D: $0.897$ vs.\ $0.636$;
E: $0.689$ vs.\ $0.455$).
This is the opposite of the single-qubit finding from \cite{Paper1Brusnikin},
where the physics prior provided a decisive advantage.
The reason is that 2-qubit coherence---even for XXZ---is not well
approximated by a simple exponential, so the OLS prior provides a
biased starting point that the LSTM residual must overcome rather than
simply correct.
Unlike standard PINNs~\cite{Raissi2019}, where a physics term enters as
a soft loss penalty the network can learn to down-weight, our residual
prior is a hard architectural constraint (Eq.~\ref{eq:residual}): when
it is structurally wrong, as it is for oscillatory 2-qubit coherence,
the network cannot simply ignore it, and can only partially compensate
through $\delta T_\mathrm{LSTM}$.
For 2-qubit systems, \emph{the pure Transformer is the recommended
architecture}.

\paragraph{Role of the adaptive prior.}
Despite the Transformer's superiority in E6a, the adaptive
\texttt{physics\_lstm} remains valuable for interpretability.
The adaptive gate isolates the regime where the OLS prior
\emph{does} apply (linear window, e.g., late-stage XXZ decay) from the
regime where it does not (oscillatory TFIM), enabling interpretable
predictions.
An earlier version of this paper read E11b as showing that
\texttt{physics\_lstm} does not benefit from more training data
(D: $0.740 \to 0.491$ on doubling to 1{,}000 trajectories) and explained
it by the richer diversity of $\gamma(t)$ profiles overwhelming the
inductive bias of the OLS prior.
That comparison was not controlled---E11b and its $500$-trajectory
baseline also differ in window length, early-stopping patience and
inference codes---so we re-ran the $500$- and $1{,}000$-trajectory
configurations against each other across three seeds, changing nothing
but the trajectory count.
The effect does not survive: paired differences are $-0.263$, $+0.287$,
$-0.192$ on XXZ and $-0.285$, $+0.094$, $-0.008$ on TFIM, giving
$\Delta R^2 = -0.056 \pm 0.299$ and $-0.066 \pm 0.196$.
The sign is not even consistent---on one seed the extra data helps
markedly---and the mean is several times smaller than the spread.
The $500$-trajectory arm is itself extremely noisy
($R^2 = 0.567 \pm 0.209$ on XXZ), which is what made a single pair of
runs look decisive.
We therefore withdraw the data-scaling claim: these experiments do not
establish that \texttt{physics\_lstm} degrades with more data, and the
Transformer recommendation rests on its accuracy and its much smaller
run-to-run spread, not on a scaling asymmetry.

\paragraph{Transformer architecture recommendations.}
Taken together, E6a (single-scenario ablation), E7a (cross-system),
and E7b ($\tau$ sweep) point to a clear deployment strategy:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{For XXZ (D):} use the Transformer when accuracy is
    paramount ($R^2 = 0.897$ single-scenario, $0.758$ cross-system);
    \texttt{physics\_lstm} remains the option when an interpretable
    single model is needed, but we cannot recommend a specific gate
    threshold (Sec.~\ref{sec:e7b}).
  \item \textit{For TFIM (E):} use a pure Transformer.
    The gate threshold is again unresolved; the only consistent
    directional signal is that the risk head tolerates a positive
    $\tau$ while regression does not clearly prefer one.
  \item \textit{Mixed deployment:} the Transformer trained on D+E
    with window $L=20$ and min-gap overlap control (E11a) achieves
    $R^2 = 0.817$ (XXZ) and AUROC $= 0.937$ (XXZ) / $0.888$ (TFIM)
    from a single checkpoint.
    This is the recommended configuration for systems where both
    regression accuracy and early-warning reliability are required.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Phase-transition anomaly.}
The E7c $J$-sweep shows predictability falling as the coupling grows.
A single run had placed that loss as a dip localised at the transition,
worst just past $J = h$ and recovering beyond it, but replication across
seeds leaves only the monotone part: weak coupling is the predictable
regime ($R^2 = 0.715 \pm 0.032$ at $J = 0.2$), everything from
$J = 0.8$ upward sits near $0.5$, and $J = h$ is not distinguishable
from its neighbours.
The practical reading changes accordingly: it is strong coupling
relative to the transverse field, not proximity to the transition, that
predicts unreliable early warning, and that is what should be factored
into error-correction scheduling policies~\cite{Lidar2013}.

\paragraph{Why does predictability fall with coupling? Two finite-size
accounts, both tested.}
In the thermodynamic limit ($N\to\infty$) the 1D transverse-field
Ising chain has a genuine quantum critical point at $J=h$, where the
excitation gap closes as $\Delta\sim|J-h|^{z\nu}$ ($z=\nu=1$) and the
correlation length diverges~\cite{Pfeuty1970,Sachdev2011}.
Our system is the $N=2$ instance of this Hamiltonian, so no true
non-analyticity can occur---finite systems never exhibit sharp phase
transitions, only smooth crossovers that sharpen into the transition
as $N\to\infty$.
We can nonetheless identify the finite-size precursor of the
transition \emph{exactly}, since the simulated Hamiltonian
$H_{\mathrm{TFIM}} = J\,\sigma_x\!\otimes\!\sigma_x
+ h(\sigma_z\otimes I + I\otimes\sigma_z)$ block-diagonalises under the
global-flip parity $P=\sigma_z\otimes\sigma_z$ (which commutes with
$H_{\mathrm{TFIM}}$) into two exactly solvable $2\times2$ sectors:
\begin{equation}
  E_{P=+1} = \pm\sqrt{J^2+4h^2}, \qquad
  E_{P=-1} = \pm J.
  \label{eq:tfim-spectrum}
\end{equation}
Both branches are smooth (analytic) functions of $J$ for all $J>0$---
confirming explicitly, for this exact model, that finite $N$ washes out
the non-analyticity, as finite-size scaling theory predicts.
What \emph{does} change qualitatively with $J$ is the ratio of the two
sectors' level splittings,
\begin{equation}
  r(J) \;=\; \frac{2J}{2\sqrt{J^2+4h^2}} \;=\; \frac{J}{\sqrt{J^2+4h^2}},
  \label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
which grows monotonically from $r\to0$ (deep paramagnetic, $J\ll h$:
the $P{=}{+}1$ sector's splitting dominates, so coherent dynamics is
governed by essentially one frequency) through $r(h)=1/\sqrt5\approx
0.447$ at the nominal transition to $r\to1$ (deep ferromagnetic,
$J\gg h$: both splittings again scale together, $\propto2J$).
An earlier version of this paper asserted at this point that the
amplitude-damping dissipator does not commute with $P$
($\sigma_x\sigma_-\sigma_x=\sigma_+$) and therefore couples the two
parity sectors. That is wrong for the system simulated here, and the
claim is withdrawn.
The jump operator $L$ enters the dissipator quadratically,
$\mathcal{D}[L]\rho = L\rho L^\dagger - \tfrac12\{L^\dagger L,\rho\}$,
so whenever $PLP^\dagger=\pm L$ the sign cancels and the parity
symmetry survives; in this basis $\sigma_-\mapsto-\sigma_-$, which is
exactly that case.
Checked numerically on the simulator itself, the Liouvillian commutes
with the parity superoperator $\rho\mapsto P\rho P^\dagger$ to machine
precision at every $J$ in the sweep, for amplitude damping and for
dephasing alike, while deliberately asymmetric jump operators (whose
image is not proportional to $L$) give commutator norms of order $30$.
The sectors do not mix.

A second, weaker account remains available at this point, and we tested it
rather than adopting it.
Parity is conserved, but a generic initial state populates both
sectors---over random pure states, $98\%$ carry more than $5\%$ of
their weight in each---so the observed coherence superposes modes at
both sectors' frequencies, whose ratio is $r(J)$.
Where one frequency dominates ($r\approx0$) the signal is effectively
single-mode; in the band $r(J)\in[0.37,0.60]$ neither dominates, and an
$\ell_1$-coherence trajectory with two comparably weighted, comparably
fast channels is harder to extrapolate from a short early window than
one---the same information limit as the single-exponential case in
\cite{Paper1Brusnikin}, with two competing modes instead of one.
Since $r(J)$ rises monotonically and is smooth at $J=h$, this predicts a
monotone decline with no feature at the transition, which is what the
replicated sweep shows.

That agreement is not evidence, because the prediction is falsifiable
directly: confine the initial state to a single parity sector and the
signal carries one frequency instead of two, so the $J$-dependence
should largely disappear.
We ran that test (Table~\ref{tab:r6}), preparing initial states by
projection onto $P=+1$ and onto $P=-1$, with the Hamiltonian,
dissipator and integration unchanged.

and stops mattering as $J$ grows: at $J=0.2$ the single-sector arms
reach $0.831$ and $0.862$ against $0.625$ for generic preparation, and
with a quarter of the spread ($\pm0.06$, $\pm0.04$ against $\pm0.26$),
while by $J\geq0.8$ the three arms are indistinguishable.
Something else dominates at strong coupling.
The overall energy scale $\sqrt{J^2+4h^2}$ grows relative to the
dissipation rate $\gamma(t)$, and the shape of the decay changes with
it; separating those is beyond a seven-point sweep and we leave it open.

The empirical finding stands on its own: predictability falls with
coupling strength and then saturates, robustly across seeds, with $J=h$
carrying no special status.
Its cause is now an open question rather than a settled one---both
explanations this paper offered have been tested and neither survived.

\paragraph{Remaining open questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Entanglement features.}
    Adding concurrence \cite{Wootters1998} or negativity as explicit
    features could further improve the model by directly exposing
    inter-qubit correlations---particularly relevant given that
    entanglement can terminate in finite time \cite{YuEberly2009}
    while single-qubit coherence decays only asymptotically.
  \item \textbf{What drives the loss at strong coupling.}
    Sector purity helps markedly at $J=0.2$ and not at all by
    $J\geq0.8$, so the strong-coupling regime is governed by something
    the spectral picture does not capture---plausibly the growth of the
    overall energy scale $\sqrt{J^2+4h^2}$ relative to $\gamma(t)$, and
    the change in decay shape that comes with it.
  \item \textbf{More seeds before a finer grid.}
    The replicated sweep leaves a monotone decline whose per-point
    spread ($\pm 0.03$--$0.17$) is comparable to the differences between
    neighbouring $J$. Resolving any remaining structure calls for more
    seeds at the present seven points rather than a denser grid, which
    would only add points of the same precision.
  \item \textbf{Extension to $n > 2$ qubits.}
    The architecture is agnostic to qubit count; scaling to 3--4 qubits
    is a natural next step.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

We have extended physics-informed early warning of quantum decoherence
to two-qubit XXZ and TFIM systems and completed a thorough experimental
programme (E6--E7).
Key findings are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \emph{learned component} eliminates the catastrophic OLS
    failure on TFIM: $R^2$ improves from $-4.27$
    (\texttt{physics\_only}) to $+0.455$ (\texttt{physics\_lstm}) in
    single-scenario training, an improvement that survives replication
    across seeds.
    We had attributed this to the adaptive prior gate
    (Eq.~\ref{eq:adaptive}), but the gain is not specific to it:
    \texttt{lstm\_only}, which carries no prior and no gate, does at
    least as well under replication, so the evidence supports the
    learned residual rather than the gate.
  \item Scaling to 500 trajectories and adding improved training
    (weighted BCE for risk head, noise augmentation, min-gap window
    sampling) pushes TFIM cross-system $R^2$ from $-0.381$ (pilot) to
    $+0.575$ ($\Delta = +0.96$) and XXZ cross-system AUROC from $0.758$
    to $0.789$.
  \item The \emph{Transformer is the recommended architecture} for
    2-qubit systems: $R^2 = 0.897$ / $0.689$ (single-scenario D/E) and
    $R^2 = 0.758$ / $0.599$ (cross-system D/E), outperforming
    \texttt{physics\_lstm} in all conditions.
    The physics prior is not advantageous when 2-qubit coherence is
    non-exponential.
  \item The \emph{gate threshold $\tau$ is not resolved} by our
    experiments (E7b). The single sweep suggests Hamiltonian-dependent
    optima, but it varies weight initialisation along with $\tau$, and a
    three-seed replication at full size ($500$~trajectories,
    $150$~epochs) leaves no detectable effect: on XXZ the means span
    $0.068$ across $\tau$ against a seed-to-seed spread of $0.085$ at
    fixed $\tau$, and on TFIM the spread across seeds ($0.397$ on
    average) is as large as the span across $\tau$ ($0.419$).
    If a $\tau$ effect exists it is below this pipeline's run-to-run
    variation.
  \item Decoherence predictability \emph{falls as the TFIM coupling
    grows and then saturates}: $R^2 = 0.715 \pm 0.032$ at $J = 0.2$
    against $\approx 0.53$ for $J \geq 0.8$ (E7c, replicated across
    three seeds). A single sweep had suggested a dip localised at the
    quantum phase transition with recovery beyond it; neither the
    location nor the recovery reproduces, and $J = h$ is not
    distinguishable from its neighbours.
    The cause is open. This paper offered two explanations and tested
    both: that the dissipator couples the Hamiltonian's parity sectors
    (the Liouvillian in fact conserves parity exactly, at every $J$),
    and that a generic initial state superposes two comparable modes,
    one per sector (confining the state to a single sector does not
    reduce the decline---see Table~\ref{tab:r6}). Neither survives, so
    we report the trend as an empirical result whose mechanism is not
    established, rather than attach an account we have falsified.
  \item \emph{Measurement noise up to $\sigma = 0.10$ does not
    systematically degrade accuracy}: paired within each seed, $R^2$
    changes by $-0.008 \pm 0.069$ and the sign varies, so noise moves
    the metric about as much as changing the seed does. This is a
    milder degradation than the single-qubit case ($\Delta R^2 = 0.23$).
    The single run's much flatter reading---a total span of $0.007$,
    with AUROC apparently improving under noise---does not replicate:
    per-seed spans are $0.072$--$0.148$ and AUROC falls in all three
    seeds, so that reading and the implicit-regularisation explanation
    for it are both withdrawn.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Reproducibility}
All reported runs are seeded end to end: the sampled system
configurations, the trajectory simulation (including each trajectory's
random initial pure state), the window sampling and train/validation/test
split, and the network initialisation and batch order.
Repeated runs of the experiment suite reproduce byte-identically for a
given state of the trajectory cache; the cache stores each simulated
trajectory under a key derived from its configuration, seed and index, so
a cold cache---the state from which an independent replication
starts---and a fully populated one are each deterministic, while a
partially populated one is not, because the initial states are drawn from
one seeded stream whose position depends on how many trajectories are
cache hits.
Results were produced with Python~3.12.1, NumPy~2.4.4, SciPy~1.17.1,
QuTiP~5.2.3 and PyTorch~2.11.0 on CPU.
Single-run tables use seed~42; every replication reported here uses seeds
$\{42,43,44\}$ and states its per-seed values so that any comparison can
be checked against its own spread.
