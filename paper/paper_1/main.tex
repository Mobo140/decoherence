\begin{abstract}
We address the problem of predicting the remaining decoherence time $T_2$
of a single qubit from a short window of Pauli expectation values,
\emph{without} knowledge of the dissipation rate $\gamma(t)$.
The window values are ensemble averages over repeated preparations, so
the setting is characterisation and calibration (prediction for subsequent
runs), not a warning during a single computation.
For constant $\gamma$ and amplitude damping, the least-squares slope of
$\log C(t)$ gives $T_2$ exactly and no learning is needed.
For unknown, time-dependent $\gamma(t)$ we combine this slope-based
estimate with a BiLSTM that learns a correction to it.
On 400 independent test trajectories per scenario, the residual model
reaches $R^2 = 0.948$ on time-dependent amplitude damping and $0.989$ on
dephasing, ahead in every seed of the same network without the estimate
(by $0.08$--$0.10$), of a Transformer (by $0.05$) and of a
stretched-exponential fit (by $0.02$--$0.05$).
Nearly all of the gain comes from the estimate as an input feature; the
residual output structure adds at most $0.01$.
The estimate is sensitive to measurement noise, and a residual model
trained on clean data inherits this; training on noisy copies of the
windows mitigates it in the tested setting: at $\sigma = 0.05$ the residual model keeps
$R^2 = 0.70$ instead of $0.31$, level with networks that do not use the
estimate, at a cost of $0.02$ on clean data.
Followed along each trajectory, the residual model predicts $T_2$ within
10\% for 90\% (amplitude damping) and nearly all (dephasing) trajectories
from half the lifetime on, and its alarm for decoherence within one time
unit fires on time for 95--100\% of them.
With a fluctuating dissipation rate it remains the most accurate model
for moderate fluctuations. Measurement noise correlated in time removes
its lead over a Transformer unless the training noise is correlated as
well; with correlated training noise it is again the most accurate model
up to $\sigma = 0.02$, at no cost on clean data.
Direct prediction outperforms
reconstruction of $\gamma(t)$ followed by simulation, which gives negative
$R^2$ at every window length.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Decoherence is the central obstacle to scalable quantum
computation~\cite{Zurek2003}.
A quantum system coupled to a Markovian environment evolves under the
Lindblad master equation~\cite{Lindblad1976,Breuer2002} ($\hbar = 1$):
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\rho} = -i[H,\rho]
  + \gamma(t)\!\left(L\rho L^\dagger
    - \tfrac{1}{2}\{L^\dagger L,\rho\}\right),
  \label{eq:lindblad}
\end{equation}
where $L$ is the jump operator and $\gamma(t) \geq 0$ is the
time-dependent dissipation rate.
Throughout this work the single-qubit Hamiltonian is
$H = \omega\sigma_z$ with $\omega$ drawn uniformly from $[0.5, 2.0]$,
and $L$ is either $\sigma_-$ (amplitude damping) or $\sigma_z$
(dephasing).
The \emph{decoherence time} $T_2$ is the time at which the
$\ell_1$-coherence $C(t) = \sum_{i \neq j}|\rho_{ij}(t)|$ falls to
$e^{-1}$ of its initial value.

Knowing the remaining decoherence time from the beginning of a decay
curve is useful for characterisation and calibration: it tells how long a
given device configuration will stay coherent in subsequent runs, so that,
for example, the recalibration interval or the circuit depth can be chosen
accordingly. The same separation in time between probing and use underlies
the prediction of qubit dephasing in the experiment of Mavadia et
al.~\cite{Mavadia2017}.

\paragraph{Setting.}
Each Pauli expectation is an \emph{ensemble average} over repeated
preparations of the same initial state, evolved to the window time and
measured. Coherence and purity are nonlinear functions computed from these
expectations, not themselves averages of single-shot outcomes. A single run gives only outcomes $\pm1$, and the
measurement changes the state, so such a window cannot be recorded along
one running instance. The use case is therefore characterisation and
calibration: the first part of the decay curve is measured on repeated
preparations, and the remaining time is predicted for the following runs.
It is not a warning issued during a single computation. Here the
averages are computed exactly from the simulated $\rho(t)$ for the original
experiments; the separate finite-shot experiment R29 uses sampled outcomes
(Sec.~\ref{sec:r29}). Acquisition cost is discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}.

\paragraph{When is ML needed?}
For constant $\gamma$ and $\sigma_-$ (amplitude damping), the coherence
decays as $C(t) = C_0\,e^{-\gamma t/2}$, so $\log C$ is linear:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\log C}{dt} = -\frac{\gamma}{2}
  \;\Longrightarrow\;
  T_2 = \frac{2}{\gamma} = -\frac{1}{\mathrm{slope}}.
  \label{eq:ols}
\end{equation}
A simple OLS fit to $\log C(t)$ over any pre-decoherence window
recovers $T_2$ exactly ($R^2 = 1.000$ in every seed, Sec.~\ref{sec:e1}).
Machine learning is \emph{unnecessary} in this regime.
Figure~\ref{fig:physics-validation} illustrates this on 60 independent
constant-$\gamma$ trajectories: predicted and true $T_2$ lie essentially on
the diagonal.

The problem becomes non-trivial when $\gamma(t)$ is \emph{unknown and
time-varying}---the realistic case on every physical platform
(Table~\ref{tab:platforms})~\cite{Paladino2014}.
In this regime, the OLS slope captures only the instantaneous
$\gamma(t)/2$, and the resulting $T_2$ estimate can be severely biased.

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.}
The closest related work, arXiv:2505.06928~\cite{TransformerLindblad2025},
uses a pure Transformer to \emph{reconstruct} $\gamma(t)$ from a
\emph{complete} trajectory via Bernstein polynomial regression.
That work solves an inverse problem; ours solves a direct prediction
problem (Table~\ref{tab:comparison}).
Its inputs are noise-free ensemble averages from QuTiP: $\langle\sigma_z\rangle$
or all three Pauli averages for one qubit, and for two qubits only
single-qubit averages; the finite number of repetitions, tomography and
the back-action of measurement are not discussed. Our inputs are of the
same kind, so the question of their accessibility applies to both: each
value requires repeated preparations at every window time
(Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}).
In Sec.~\ref{sec:e5} we directly compare the two approaches on the same
partial-window setting and show that the inverse approach is
ill-conditioned at short windows (mean $R^2$ over three seeds from $-0.44$
to $-2.18$ across window fractions $f \leq 0.50$), while direct prediction
remains reliable (mean $R^2 = 0.95$--$0.97$).

Other work differs in what is observed. Luchnikov et
al.~\cite{Luchnikov2020} learn non-Markovian dynamics from a single long
record of projective measurements in random bases on one instance of the
system, with the back-action of each measurement included in the
likelihood; this is the opposite of the repeated identical preparations
that our inputs need. Teretenkov, Kuznetsov and Pechen~\cite{Teretenkov2026}
fix the algebra of observables that can actually be measured and learn the
probabilities of multi-time outcome sequences, from which a generator is
fitted; for settings where only sequential measurements are available,
this is the framework to which our problem should be transferred.
Mavadia et al.~\cite{Mavadia2017} predict the future phase of a trapped-ion
qubit from past Ramsey phase estimates, with probing and compensation
separated in time, which is the closest experimental analogue of our
scenario; they predict a phase correction, not the time at which coherence
crosses a threshold. Maan et al.~\cite{Maan2026} predict the drift of the
spectral line of a single-photon emitter from spectra that are measured
continuously, that is, a classical signal that is actually available in time;
what is predicted is a parameter of the environment, not the time to a
threshold, and a closed-loop compensation was not realised. Abu-Nada,
Banerjee and Wu~\cite{AbuNada2026} predict the next value of
$\langle\sigma_x\rangle$ for a dephased qubit under telegraph noise to
trigger decoupling pulses; their input is also an average over noise
trajectories, and how it is measured is not discussed.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Simulation setup}
\label{sec:sim}

We study three single-qubit scenarios (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}).
The dissipation rate $\gamma(t)$ is parameterised by degree-4 Bernstein
polynomials with non-negative coefficients, which guarantees
$\gamma(t) \geq 0$ by construction.
Scenarios~B and~C cycle through six profile shapes---random, monotone
increasing, monotone decreasing, peak, step-up and step-down---while
scenario~A uses a constant rate.
The coefficient range differs by channel, since the two dissipators give
different $T_2$ for the same rate: $\gamma \leq 0.8$ for amplitude
damping ($T_2 \approx 2/\gamma$) and $\gamma \leq 0.2$ for dephasing
($T_2 = 1/2\gamma$), so that $T_2 \gtrsim 2.5$ in both.
Unless stated otherwise, each scenario uses 100 randomly sampled configurations, integrated on a
grid of step $dt = 0.1$ up to $t_\mathrm{max} = 20$ ($30$ for
scenario~A, whose slowest configurations decohere later), via the
Lindblad solver in \textsc{QuTiP}~\cite{qutip}.
Each trajectory contributes up to five windows with prediction indices sampled
without replacement strictly before $T_2$. For index $e$ the input is
$\mathbf w=(x_{e-W},\ldots,x_{e-1})$, whereas the prediction time is
$t_\mathrm{obs}=t_e$; the last observation is therefore one grid step
($dt=0.1$) earlier. Windows cannot be formed if too little history is
available, and trajectories without a threshold crossing are excluded from
the reported standard experiments.

\subsection{Cost of data acquisition}
\label{sec:cost}

Each window value is an average over $N$ repeated preparations, measured
in $S$ settings at each of $L$ window time points, so a window costs
$N\times S\times L$ shots. For one qubit $S=3$ ($\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z$),
which also gives coherence and purity; for full two-qubit tomography
$S=9$, while three local settings give only the six single-qubit means.
An estimated expectation value is the mean of $N$ outcomes $\pm1$ and
has binomial standard deviation
$\sigma \approx \sqrt{(1-\langle\sigma\rangle^2)/N}$.
As an arithmetic illustration only, $N=1000$, $S=3$ and $L=50$ give
$1.5\cdot10^{5}$ shots for one window. The original E and R1--R27 results use exact
averages, except for their explicitly simulated additive-noise tests; R29
separately evaluates finite $N$ in Sec.~\ref{sec:r29}.

\subsection{Physics-informed residual architecture}

The OLS-slope formula~(\ref{eq:ols}) provides a useful starting point
even for time-dependent $\gamma(t)$: it encodes the \emph{current}
decay rate from the observation window.
We therefore structure the model as a residual corrector:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{T}_{2,\text{rem}}
  = \underbrace{T_{\mathrm{physics}}(\mathbf{w})}_{\text{OLS estimate}}
  + \underbrace{\delta T_{\mathrm{LSTM}}(\mathbf{w})}_{\text{learned correction}},
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{W \times F}$ is the observation window.
This design has two properties:
(i)~\emph{analytical limit}: if the correction is zero, the model reduces
to the physical estimate in Eq.~(\ref{eq:ols}); training does not enforce a
zero correction, and the learned model need not recover the analytic limit
exactly; (ii)~\emph{corrective capacity}: when $\gamma(t)$ varies, the LSTM
learns a residual, which can be compared empirically with direct regression.

\paragraph{Network details.}
A bidirectional LSTM~\cite{HochreiterSchmidhuber1997} (hidden size 64,
1 layer) processes $\mathbf{w}$---the three Pauli expectations,
$\ell_1$-coherence and purity---augmented with $\log C$ and
$\log(\mathrm{purity})$, so $F = 7$ input channels.
The mean-pooled output is concatenated with seven scalar inputs
$(t_\text{obs},\text{slope},\log C_\text{obs},T_\text{physics},
R^2_\text{OLS}$, and two categorical codes identifying the Hamiltonian
and the jump operator$)$, yielding a 135-dimensional representation fed
into two heads, $\mathrm{Lin}(64)\to\mathrm{GELU}\to\mathrm{Lin}(1)$ for
the correction $\delta T$ and $\mathrm{Lin}(32)\to\mathrm{GELU}\to
\mathrm{Lin}(1)$ for an auxiliary classification of decoherence within
the horizon $\Delta t = 1$, which enters only the training loss.
At prediction time the alarm score of every model is a logistic function
of its predicted remaining time,
$p = 1/\bigl(1+e^{5(\widehat T_{2,\text{rem}}-\Delta t)}\bigr)$, so AUROC
measures how well the predicted remaining time ranks windows with
respect to the event $T_2 - t_\mathrm{obs} \le \Delta t$.
Inputs and the regression target are standardised with training-set
statistics; the scale of $R^2_\text{OLS}$, which is nearly constant on
clean single-qubit windows, is floored at $0.05$.
Training uses AdamW~\cite{LoshchilovHutter2019} (learning rate
$10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-3}$, batch size 32, at most 100 epochs)
with Huber regression loss and BCE classification loss, a
\texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} schedule and patience-based early stopping on
validation loss.
Unless stated otherwise the observation window is $W = 20$ steps.

\paragraph{Ablation variants.}
\texttt{physics\_only}: OLS formula, no neural network.
\texttt{stretched\_exp}: fit of $C(t)=C_0\exp(-(t/\tau)^\beta)$ over the
window, $T_2=\tau$, no learning.
\texttt{lstm\_only}: the same BiLSTM with $T_\text{physics}$ and the slope
set to zero, predicting the whole remaining time; the other scalars, including
$\log C$, $R^2_\text{OLS}$ and the scenario codes, remain inputs.
\texttt{physics\_feature}: the same BiLSTM receiving $T_\text{physics}$ as
an input scalar but predicting the whole remaining time (no residual
output).
\texttt{physics\_lstm}: full residual model (ours).
\texttt{transformer}: pure Transformer encoder over the five raw channels,
without the two log channels or physics scalars. The current implementation
projects them to dimension 64, adds sinusoidal position encoding, and uses
two encoder layers with four attention heads and feed-forward dimension 256,
followed by mean pooling and two heads. This is analogous to
arXiv:2505.06928 applied to the $T_2$ task, but does not reconstruct $\gamma$.

\section{Experiments}

Regression is scored by $R^2$ and MAE of the predicted decoherence time
$\widehat T_2 = t_\mathrm{obs} + \widehat T_{2,\text{rem}}$ against the
true $T_2$ over all test windows; the alarm by AUROC~\cite{Fawcett2006}.
Unless stated otherwise, replication results are mean $\pm$ s.d.\ over seeds $\{42,43,44\}$; a seed fixes the sampled configurations, the split and the
network initialisation, and comparisons are made as paired differences
within a seed.
With 100 trajectories per scenario the test split holds 10 trajectories
(50 windows), on which even predicting the training mean gives $R^2$
between $-2.0$ and $0.0$; Sec.~\ref{sec:r13} therefore scores the same
models on a large independent test set.

\subsection{How early the prediction is reliable}
\label{sec:r18}

The metrics above average over windows whose prediction index is drawn
uniformly before $T_2$. To see at which stage of the decay the prediction becomes
reliable, we train the models as in E1 and follow 200 fresh trajectories
per scenario step by step: a prediction is made at every grid point from

\paragraph{Noise-aware training.}
A better estimate of the slope from the same window does not help:
weighted least squares of $\log C$, a nonlinear exponential fit, SSA
denoising and Holt's exponential smoothing all collapse like the plain
OLS estimate at $\sigma \ge 0.05$ (experiment R17, no training). The
problem is the estimator $-1/\hat s$ itself: on slowly decaying windows the
change of $\log C$ is comparable to the noise, and dividing by a small
slope amplifies any error.
In clean-data training the physics scalars are computed from clean windows, so
the network never learns how far to trust $T_\text{physics}$ on a noisy
window. We therefore extend the training and validation sets with two
noisy copies of every window ($\sigma$ drawn log-uniformly from
$[0.005, 0.12]$) and compute every input---the window and the slope, OLS
$R^2$ and $T_\text{physics}$ derived from it---from the noisy copy.
These R16 copies are distinct from the BiLSTM constructor option
\texttt{augment\_sigma}, which adds noise inside the training step without
recomputing the previously prepared physics scalars. The Transformer does
not receive those scalars. Test windows are as in
Table~\ref{tab:e3} (Table~\ref{tab:r16}, Fig.~\ref{fig:r16}).

\subsection{Correlated noise and a fluctuating dissipation rate}
\label{sec:r21}

Two assumptions behind the results above are relaxed here (experiment
R21; training as in Sec.~\ref{sec:e3}, three seeds).

\paragraph{Correlated measurement noise.}
Drifts in readout calibration make noise correlated in time. We replace
the independent noise by a stationary AR(1) process with the same
marginal $\sigma$ and lag-one correlation $\varphi \in \{0, 0.5, 0.9\}$;
one realisation per seed is filtered for every $\varphi$, so the
comparison is paired, and $\varphi = 0$ reproduces Table~\ref{tab:e3}.
Over a window of $n = 50$ points AR(1) errors inflate the variance of the
OLS slope by a factor of $2.8$ ($\varphi = 0.5$) and $9.6$
($\varphi = 0.9$), against $(1+\varphi)/(1-\varphi) = 3$ and $19$ for
$n \to \infty$: the noise is low-frequency and looks like a trend
(Fig.~\ref{fig:r21}a).
At $\varphi = 0.5$ every model changes by less than $0.04$.
At $\varphi = 0.9$ and $\sigma = 0.05$ every trained network loses in
every seed: the Transformer $0.091 \pm 0.044$, the noise-aware residual
model $0.109 \pm 0.083$, \texttt{lstm\_only} $0.022 \pm 0.024$.
Noise-aware training with white noise still transfers in part: it keeps
the residual model $0.145 \pm 0.066$ above the same model trained on
clean data, in every seed. Its lead over the Transformer, however, is
gone (from $+0.042$ to $+0.016 \pm 0.023$ at $\sigma = 0.02$; from
$-0.021$ to $-0.040 \pm 0.048$ at $\sigma = 0.05$, sign varying).
The residual model trained on clean data gains from correlation
($+0.135 \pm 0.072$ at $\sigma = 0.05$, every seed), but remains far
below the noise-aware one; correlated noise changes less from step to
step (increments have standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{2(1-\varphi)}$), so
the window looks smoother to a network that has seen only clean data.

\paragraph{Stochastic dissipation rate.}
A real bath fluctuates. We multiply the Bernstein rate by a log-normal
factor,
\begin{equation}
  \gamma(t) = \gamma_B(t)\, e^{s\,x(t) - s^2/2},
  \label{eq:ou}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is a stationary Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process with unit variance
and correlation time $\tau$; the factor has mean one, so the mean rate is
unchanged. Models are evaluated on 200 independent trajectories per seed
for $s \in \{0, 0.3, 0.6\}$ and $\tau \in \{0.5, 2\}$, with the same
initial states and driving noise at every setting, so $s = 0$ is a
paired control. A further arm trains the residual model and the
Transformer on trajectories with $s \sim U[0, 0.6]$ and
$\tau \in \{0.5, 2\}$ (Table~\ref{tab:r21}, Fig.~\ref{fig:r21}b).

expected: what decides $T_2$ is the rate integrated over the remaining
lifetime $T$, and the variance of $\int_0^T x\,dt$,
$2\tau T - 2\tau^2(1 - e^{-T/\tau})$, grows with $\tau$ while
$\tau \lesssim T$; fast fluctuations average out, slow ones do not.
The residual model stays best at $s = 0.3$ ($+0.033 \pm 0.022$ and
$+0.022 \pm 0.017$ over the Transformer, every seed, against $+0.087$ at
$s = 0$) and is level with it at $s = 0.6$ (sign varying).
Training on stochastic trajectories does not help: the change is
$-0.010$ to $+0.017$ for the residual model (sign varying) and negative
in every seed for the Transformer. The loss is therefore not a shift
between training and test distributions but a limit on predictability:
the window does not contain the future fluctuations of the rate.

\subsection{E4 --- One model for all three scenarios}
\label{sec:e4}

A single \texttt{physics\_lstm} trained on A+B+C reaches
$R^2 = 0.835 \pm 0.101$ on B and $0.978 \pm 0.004$ on C, but only
$0.452 \pm 0.082$ on A, where the formula is exact: the correct correction there is zero, and the
mixture-trained network applies a non-zero one. A regime classifier
selecting \texttt{physics\_only} when $\log C$ is linear over the window
would remove this loss.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What the physics estimate contributes.}
The slope-based estimate is the most useful single input for this task:
with it the BiLSTM gains $0.08$--$0.10$ in $R^2$ and becomes the best
model, ahead of a Transformer and of a classical fit. The gain comes from
the estimate itself rather than from the residual output: a network that
receives the estimate as a feature and predicts the whole remaining time
does almost as well. What the residual structure adds is the analytic
limit---a correction that can be zero---but a network trained on a mixture
of regimes does not reliably learn to make it zero (Sec.~\ref{sec:e4}).
Unlike a PINN~\cite{Raissi2019}, where physics enters as a soft penalty,
the estimate here enters as an input and as part of the output; the
price is that its errors propagate, which makes a model trained on clean
data sensitive to measurement noise (Sec.~\ref{sec:e3}). Training on noisy
windows improves robustness in the tested noise distribution at a small cost
on clean data, but does not establish robustness to arbitrary measurement noise.

\paragraph{Risk score as a practical interface.}
The alarm score is a monotone function of the predicted remaining time,
and AUROC $\approx 0.98$ means that the predicted remaining time ranks
windows well with respect to imminent decoherence even where $R^2$ is
moderate.
This decoupling matters in use: a calibration routine only needs a
ranking of whether a configuration will lose coherence within the horizon.
The logistic score is not established as a calibrated probability.
The alarm needs only a short window (AUROC $0.979$ with five steps),
which keeps the number of window time points, and with it the acquisition
cost (Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}), small.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The original experiments were conducted on simulated data with 100 trajectories
per scenario (500 in Sec.~\ref{sec:r11}); the built-in test splits of 10
trajectories resolve only the larger differences, which is why the main
comparison is made on an independent test set. Measurement noise is
modelled as Gaussian noise added to every channel separately, white or
correlated in time (Sec.~\ref{sec:r21}), whereas on hardware the coherence
and purity would be computed from the noisy expectations and their errors
would be correlated across channels. R29 addresses finite-shot noise for a
specific single-qubit measurement protocol, not every hardware error source. The correlation of the training
noise has to be chosen in advance; a mismatch with the hardware noise
costs accuracy (Sec.~\ref{sec:r21}).
Validation on real hardware, where $\gamma(t)$ is neither a Bernstein
polynomial nor the log-normal process of Eq.~\ref{eq:ou}, is left for
future work. Two interacting qubits, where entanglement and
non-exponential coherence decay pose qualitatively different challenges,
are treated in a companion paper.

\section{Conclusion}

We have studied the early prediction of single-qubit decoherence under
unknown time-dependent dissipation.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For constant $\gamma$ the slope of $\log C$ gives $T_2$ exactly;
    learning is not needed.
  \item For time-dependent $\gamma$ a BiLSTM that uses the slope-based
    estimate is the most accurate model: $R^2 = 0.948$ (amplitude
    damping) and $0.989$ (dephasing) on 400 independent test trajectories
    per scenario, ahead in every seed of the same network without the
    estimate, of a Transformer and of a stretched-exponential fit.
  \item Nearly all of this gain comes from the estimate as an input
    feature; the residual output structure adds at most $0.01$.
  \item The estimate is sensitive to measurement noise, and smoothing
    the window does not cure it; training on noisy windows does: the
    residual model then stays best up to $\sigma = 0.02$ and level with
    networks without the estimate beyond, instead of collapsing.
  \item The prediction is reliable early: from half the lifetime on the
    residual model predicts $T_2$ within 10\% for 90\%--100\% of the
    trajectories, and its alarm fires on time for 95\%--100\% of them
    (Sec.~\ref{sec:r18}).
  \item With a fluctuating dissipation rate all models lose accuracy, the
    more so for slow fluctuations; the residual model stays best at
    moderate strength, and training on fluctuating rates does not help,
    because the window does not contain the future fluctuations. Noise
    correlated in time costs every network up to $0.1$ in $R^2$ at
    $\sigma = 0.05$; training with white noise does not prevent this,
    training with correlated noise does, at no cost on clean data
    (Sec.~\ref{sec:r21}).
  \item Reconstructing $\gamma(t)$ from a short window and integrating it
    is ill-conditioned ($R^2 < 0$ at every window length); direct
    prediction is not.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Data availability}
All data in this paper are simulated; no experimental data were used.
Every table is produced by the accompanying code from the trajectory
generator described in Sec.~\ref{sec:sim}, and the result files behind
each table are retained alongside it.
Reproduction also requires the recorded code version, dependencies,
configuration and random-number streams. R29 is an additional, later
experiment; its saved CSV, provenance and script are in the experiment
worktree and the study archive.

\section*{Code availability}
The simulation, training, evaluation and figure-generating code for the
original experiments is available at
\url{https://github.com/Mobo140/decoherence}.
Correlated noise and the stochastic dissipation rate are experiment R21
there, and training with correlated noise R22.

\section*{Reproducibility}
All reported runs are seeded end to end: the sampled system
configurations, the trajectory simulation (including each trajectory's
random initial pure state), the window sampling and train/validation/test
split, and the network initialisation and batch order.
The present code uses a process-local trajectory cache. Historical runs
may have used a different cache and code revision, so a seed alone does not
establish byte-identical reproduction of the saved tables.
Results were produced with Python~3.12.1, NumPy~2.4.4, SciPy~1.17.1,
QuTiP~5.2.3 and PyTorch~2.11.0 on CPU.
The historical single-run tables use seed~42; replications generally use seeds
$\{42,43,44\}$ (R22 and R29: $42$--$46$); per-seed values are
retained in the corresponding CSV files.
BiLSTM training was bit-identical in repeated runs under the tested
environment. Transformer training on
CPU additionally depends on the number of threads through the order of
floating-point summation; reruns of the same seed with a different
thread count differ by up to $0.02$ in $R^2$, below the seed-to-seed
spread of every comparison in which the Transformer enters.
