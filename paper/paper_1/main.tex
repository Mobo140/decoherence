\begin{abstract}
We address the problem of predicting the remaining decoherence time $T_2$
of a single qubit from a short window of Pauli expectation values,
\emph{without} knowledge of the dissipation rate $\gamma(t)$.
For constant $\gamma$ and amplitude damping, the least-squares slope of
$\log C(t)$ gives $T_2$ exactly and no learning is needed.
For unknown, time-dependent $\gamma(t)$ we combine this slope-based
estimate with a BiLSTM that learns a correction to it.
On 400 independent test trajectories per scenario, the residual model
reaches $R^2 = 0.948$ on time-dependent amplitude damping and $0.989$ on
dephasing, ahead in every seed of the same network without the estimate
(by $0.08$--$0.10$), of a Transformer (by $0.05$) and of a
stretched-exponential fit (by $0.02$--$0.05$).
Nearly all of the gain comes from the estimate as an input feature; the
residual output structure adds at most $0.01$.
The estimate is, however, sensitive to measurement noise: from
$\sigma = 0.02$ on, networks that do not use it are more accurate.
An alarm for decoherence within one time unit reaches AUROC $\approx0.98$
with windows as short as five steps, and direct prediction outperforms
reconstruction of $\gamma(t)$ followed by simulation, which gives negative
$R^2$ at every window length.
\end{abstract}

% --------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Introduction}

Decoherence is the central obstacle to scalable quantum
computation~\cite{Zurek2003}.
A quantum system coupled to a Markovian environment evolves under the
Lindblad master equation~\cite{Lindblad1976,Breuer2002} ($\hbar = 1$):
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\rho} = -i[H,\rho]
  + \gamma(t)\!\left(L\rho L^\dagger
    - \tfrac{1}{2}\{L^\dagger L,\rho\}\right),
  \label{eq:lindblad}
\end{equation}
where $L$ is the jump operator and $\gamma(t) \geq 0$ is the
time-dependent dissipation rate.
Throughout this work the single-qubit Hamiltonian is
$H = \omega\sigma_z$ with $\omega$ drawn uniformly from $[0.5, 2.0]$,
and $L$ is either $\sigma_-$ (amplitude damping) or $\sigma_z$
(dephasing).
The \emph{decoherence time} $T_2$ is the time at which the
$\ell_1$-coherence $C(t) = \sum_{i \neq j}|\rho_{ij}(t)|$ falls to
$e^{-1}$ of its initial value.

Knowing the remaining decoherence time from a partial trajectory is
directly actionable: it enables error-correction protocols to
preemptively reset or re-encode qubits before fidelity falls below a
critical threshold~\cite{Lidar2013}.

\paragraph{When is ML needed?}
For constant $\gamma$ and $\sigma_-$ (amplitude damping), the coherence
decays as $C(t) = C_0\,e^{-\gamma t/2}$, so $\log C$ is linear:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\log C}{dt} = -\frac{\gamma}{2}
  \;\Longrightarrow\;
  T_2 = \frac{2}{\gamma} = -\frac{1}{\mathrm{slope}}.
  \label{eq:ols}
\end{equation}
A simple OLS fit to $\log C(t)$ over any pre-decoherence window
recovers $T_2$ exactly ($R^2 = 1.000$ in every seed, Sec.~\ref{sec:e1}).
Machine learning is \emph{unnecessary} in this regime.
Figure~\ref{fig:physics-validation} illustrates this on 60 independent
constant-$\gamma$ trajectories: predicted and true $T_2$ lie essentially on
the diagonal.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_physics_validation}
  \caption{Illustrative validation of the OLS-slope formula
    (Eq.~\ref{eq:ols}) on 60 fresh constant-$\gamma$ trajectories,
    independent of the E1 test set ($R^2\approx1.000$, as on the E1

  \label{fig:physics-validation}
\end{figure}

The problem becomes non-trivial when $\gamma(t)$ is \emph{unknown and
time-varying}---the realistic case on every physical platform
(Table~\ref{tab:platforms})~\cite{Paladino2014}.
In this regime, the OLS slope captures only the instantaneous
$\gamma(t)/2$, and the resulting $T_2$ estimate can be severely biased.

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.}
The closest related work, arXiv:2505.06928~\cite{TransformerLindblad2025},
uses a pure Transformer to \emph{reconstruct} $\gamma(t)$ from a
\emph{complete} trajectory via Bernstein polynomial regression.
That work solves an offline inverse problem; ours solves an online
forward problem (Table~\ref{tab:comparison}).
In Sec.~\ref{sec:e5} we directly compare the two approaches on the same
partial-window setting and show that the inverse approach is
ill-conditioned at short windows ($R^2 < -0.83$ for all $f \leq 0.50$),
while direct prediction remains reliable ($R^2 = 0.92$--$0.96$).

\section{Method}

\subsection{Simulation setup}
\label{sec:sim}

We study three single-qubit scenarios (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}).
The dissipation rate $\gamma(t)$ is parameterised by degree-4 Bernstein
polynomials with non-negative coefficients, which guarantees
$\gamma(t) \geq 0$ by construction.
Scenarios~B and~C cycle through six profile shapes---random, monotone
increasing, monotone decreasing, peak, step-up and step-down---while
scenario~A uses a constant rate.
The coefficient range differs by channel, since the two dissipators give
different $T_2$ for the same rate: $\gamma \leq 0.8$ for amplitude
damping ($T_2 \approx 2/\gamma$) and $\gamma \leq 0.2$ for dephasing
($T_2 = 1/2\gamma$), so that $T_2 \gtrsim 2.5$ in both.
Each scenario uses 100 randomly sampled configurations, integrated on a
grid of step $dt = 0.1$ up to $t_\mathrm{max} = 20$ ($30$ for
scenario~A, whose slowest configurations decohere later), via the
Lindblad solver in \textsc{QuTiP}~\cite{qutip}.
Each trajectory contributes five windows, sampled at random end times
strictly before $T_2$.

\subsection{Physics-informed residual architecture}

The OLS-slope formula~(\ref{eq:ols}) provides a useful starting point
even for time-dependent $\gamma(t)$: it encodes the \emph{current}
decay rate from the observation window.
We therefore structure the model as a residual corrector:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{T}_{2,\text{rem}}
  = \underbrace{T_{\mathrm{physics}}(\mathbf{w})}_{\text{OLS estimate}}
  + \underbrace{\delta T_{\mathrm{LSTM}}(\mathbf{w})}_{\text{learned correction}},
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{W \times F}$ is the observation window.
This design has two properties:
(i)~\emph{analytical recovery}: when $\gamma$ is constant the network
learns $\delta T \approx 0$, exactly recovering Eq.~(\ref{eq:ols});
(ii)~\emph{corrective capacity}: when $\gamma(t)$ varies, the LSTM
corrects only the residual---a much easier regression target than
predicting $T_2$ from scratch.

\paragraph{Network details.}
A bidirectional LSTM~\cite{HochreiterSchmidhuber1997} (hidden size 64,
1 layer) processes $\mathbf{w}$---the three Pauli expectations,
$\ell_1$-coherence and purity---augmented with $\log C$ and
$\log(\mathrm{purity})$, so $F = 7$ input channels.
The mean-pooled output is concatenated with seven physics scalars
$(t_\text{obs},\text{slope},\log C_\text{obs},T_\text{physics},
R^2_\text{OLS}$, and two categorical codes identifying the Hamiltonian
and the jump operator$)$, yielding a 135-dimensional representation fed
into two heads, $\mathrm{Lin}(64)\to\mathrm{GELU}\to\mathrm{Lin}(1)$ for
the correction $\delta T$ and $\mathrm{Lin}(32)\to\mathrm{GELU}\to
\mathrm{Lin}(1)$ for an auxiliary classification of decoherence within
the horizon $\Delta t = 1$, which enters only the training loss.
At prediction time the alarm score of every model is a logistic function
of its predicted remaining time,
$p = 1/\bigl(1+e^{5(\widehat T_{2,\text{rem}}-\Delta t)}\bigr)$, so AUROC
measures how well the predicted remaining time ranks windows with
respect to the event $T_2 - t_\mathrm{obs} \le \Delta t$.
Inputs and the regression target are standardised with training-set
statistics; the scale of $R^2_\text{OLS}$, which is nearly constant on
clean single-qubit windows, is floored at $0.05$.
Training uses AdamW~\cite{LoshchilovHutter2019} (learning rate
$10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-3}$, batch size 32, at most 100 epochs)
with Huber regression loss and BCE classification loss, a
\texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} schedule and patience-based early stopping on
validation loss.
Unless stated otherwise the observation window is $W = 20$ steps.

\paragraph{Ablation variants.}
\texttt{physics\_only}: OLS formula, no neural network.
\texttt{stretched\_exp}: fit of $C(t)=C_0\exp(-(t/\tau)^\beta)$ over the
window, $T_2=\tau$, no learning.
\texttt{lstm\_only}: the same BiLSTM with $T_\text{physics}$ and the slope
set to zero, predicting the whole remaining time.
\texttt{physics\_feature}: the same BiLSTM receiving $T_\text{physics}$ as
an input scalar but predicting the whole remaining time (no residual
output).
\texttt{physics\_lstm}: full residual model (ours).
\texttt{transformer}: pure Transformer encoder over the same channels, no
physics scalars, analogous to arXiv:2505.06928 applied to the $T_2$
task.

\section{Experiments}

Regression is scored by $R^2$ and MAE of the predicted decoherence time
$\widehat T_2 = t_\mathrm{obs} + \widehat T_{2,\text{rem}}$ against the
true $T_2$ over all test windows; the alarm by AUROC~\cite{Fawcett2006}.
Unless marked as single runs, results are mean $\pm$ s.d.\ over seeds
$\{42,43,44\}$; a seed fixes the sampled configurations, the split and the
network initialisation, and comparisons are made as paired differences
within a seed.
With 100 trajectories per scenario the test split holds 10 trajectories
(50 windows), on which even predicting the training mean gives $R^2$
between $-2.0$ and $0.0$; Sec.~\ref{sec:r13} therefore scores the same
models on a large independent test set.

\subsection{E2 --- Minimum observation window}
\label{sec:e2}

\texttt{physics\_lstm} is trained on a mixture of scenarios B and C
(120 trajectories each) for window lengths
$L = \max\bigl(5,\ \mathrm{round}(f\,\mathrm{median}(T_2)/dt)\bigr)$

how early the prediction is made.

does not answer.

The slope-based estimate is fragile: on a slowly decaying window the

\subsection{E4 --- One model for all three scenarios}
\label{sec:e4}

A single \texttt{physics\_lstm} trained on A+B+C (single run) reaches
$R^2 = 0.919$ on B and $0.980$ on C, but only $0.546$ on A, where the
formula is exact: the correct correction there is zero, and the
mixture-trained network applies a non-zero one. A regime classifier
selecting \texttt{physics\_only} when $\log C$ is linear over the window
would remove this loss.

\subsection{E5 --- Direct prediction vs.\ inverse-problem baseline}
\label{sec:e5}

arXiv:2505.06928 operationalised for the $T_2$ task: (1) an MLP
reconstructs the Bernstein coefficients of $\gamma(t)$ from the window;

read off $T_2$. Both use the same windows of scenario~B (single run,
Table~\ref{tab:e5}).

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What the physics estimate contributes.}
The slope-based estimate is the most useful single input for this task:
with it the BiLSTM gains $0.08$--$0.10$ in $R^2$ and becomes the best
model, ahead of a Transformer and of a classical fit. The gain comes from
the estimate itself rather than from the residual output: a network that
receives the estimate as a feature and predicts the whole remaining time
does almost as well. What the residual structure adds is the analytic
limit---a correction that can be zero---but a network trained on a mixture
of regimes does not reliably learn to make it zero (Sec.~\ref{sec:e4}).
Unlike a PINN~\cite{Raissi2019}, where physics enters as a soft penalty,
the estimate here enters as an input and as part of the output; the
price is that its errors propagate, which is what makes the model
sensitive to measurement noise (Sec.~\ref{sec:e3}).

\paragraph{Risk score as a practical interface.}
The alarm score is a monotone function of the predicted remaining time,
and AUROC $\approx 0.98$ means that the predicted remaining time ranks
windows well with respect to imminent decoherence even where $R^2$ is
moderate.
This decoupling matters for deployment: a quantum error-correction
scheduler only needs a calibrated probability estimate to decide whether
to reset a qubit, not a precise $T_2$ value.
The alarm needs only a short window (AUROC $0.979$ with five steps),
which suits real-time integration.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The experiments were conducted on simulated data with 100 trajectories
per scenario (500 in Sec.~\ref{sec:r11}); the built-in test splits of 10
trajectories resolve only the larger differences, which is why the main
comparison is made on an independent test set. Measurement noise is
modelled as independent Gaussian noise on every channel, whereas on
hardware the coherence and purity would be computed from the noisy
expectations and their errors would be correlated.
Validation on real hardware (where $\gamma(t)$ is not parameterisable
by Bernstein polynomials and readout errors are correlated) is left for
future work, as is extension to multi-qubit systems, where entanglement
and non-exponential coherence decay pose qualitatively different
challenges.

\section{Conclusion}

We have studied the early prediction of single-qubit decoherence under
unknown time-dependent dissipation.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For constant $\gamma$ the slope of $\log C$ gives $T_2$ exactly;
    learning is not needed.
  \item For time-dependent $\gamma$ a BiLSTM that uses the slope-based
    estimate is the most accurate model: $R^2 = 0.948$ (amplitude
    damping) and $0.989$ (dephasing) on 400 independent test trajectories
    per scenario, ahead in every seed of the same network without the
    estimate, of a Transformer and of a stretched-exponential fit.
  \item Nearly all of this gain comes from the estimate as an input
    feature; the residual output structure adds at most $0.01$.
  \item The estimate is sensitive to measurement noise; from
    $\sigma = 0.02$ on, networks that do not use it are more accurate,
    which calls for a noise-robust estimate before deployment on
    hardware.
  \item The alarm reaches AUROC $\approx 0.98$ with windows of five steps.
  \item Reconstructing $\gamma(t)$ from a short window and integrating it
    is ill-conditioned ($R^2 < 0$ at every window length); direct
    prediction is not.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Reproducibility}
All reported runs are seeded end to end: the sampled system
configurations, the trajectory simulation (including each trajectory's
random initial pure state), the window sampling and train/validation/test
split, and the network initialisation and batch order.
Repeated runs of the experiment suite reproduce byte-identically for a
given state of the trajectory cache; the cache stores each simulated
trajectory under a key derived from its configuration, seed and index, so
a cold cache---the state from which an independent replication
starts---and a fully populated one are each deterministic, while a
partially populated one is not, because the initial states are drawn from
one seeded stream whose position depends on how many trajectories are
cache hits.
Results were produced with Python~3.12.1, NumPy~2.4.4, SciPy~1.17.1,
QuTiP~5.2.3 and PyTorch~2.11.0 on CPU.
Single-run tables use seed~42; every replication reported here uses seeds
$\{42,43,44\}$ and states its per-seed values so that any comparison can
be checked against its own spread.
